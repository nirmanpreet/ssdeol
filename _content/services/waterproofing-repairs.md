## Fix the cause, then paint

Water finds a path. If you seal the surface without stopping the water, it comes
back — usually somewhere slightly to the side, and usually a little later.

So the rule is straightforward: **identify the water, stop it, let the substrate
dry, then coat.** That is often more expensive than just painting. It is also the
only way the paint lasts.

## What we repair

- **Failed membrane and flashing** around windows, doors, parapets and roof edges
- **Deteriorated panel joint sealant** on tilt-up and panel construction
- **Cracked render** — properly opened and repaired, not filled over
- **Water ingress around openings** — doors, windows, vents, penetrations
- **Failed coatings over wet substrates** — stripped, the cause fixed, recoated
- **Concrete defects** — spalling, delamination, exposed corroded reinforcement
- **Failed caulking and joint movement** in painted surfaces

## Heritage-appropriate work

On heritage and heritage-sensitive commercial buildings, the wrong coating does
real damage. Hard, impermeable modern coatings can trap moisture in the original
wall and accelerate its decay.

Our approach is to:

- **Match the existing colour** rather than impose a new one
- **Respect the original surface** — what was a lime or masonry finish stays
  compatible
- **Use breathable systems** where the substrate requires them
- **Work to the permit** where the building is heritage-listed, in
  collaboration with the conservation architect or heritage consultant

## Concrete repair

Where reinforcement has corroded and concrete has spalled, the repair sequence
is always the same:

1. Remove the loose and delaminated concrete
2. Assess the reinforcing steel — clean it, or replace it
3. Reinstate with an appropriate repair mortar
4. Allow to cure
5. Coat, if a coating is required

Coating over unrepaired concrete cancer guarantees you are back within a few
years, at greater cost.
